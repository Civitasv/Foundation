# From Turing Machines to Large Language Models

## Abstract

Large language models let many tasks share one interface: supply text and generate text in return. Explaining this versatility requires examining learning objectives, representations, and computational resources together. Drawing on representative work in computation theory, neural language modeling, pretraining, and scaling, this review considers how rules come to be learned from data, how language prediction supports transfer, and why data and compute become part of model design. Its central argument is that these conditions work together; none explains model capability on its own. The final sections examine the distance between prediction quality and reliable execution when language models become part of agent systems.

## Introduction: where does versatility come from?

Translating a sentence, summarizing an article, and fixing code from an error report once typically called for separate processing pipelines. A large language model can serve all three, with the task described in its input. Why should a system trained to predict language be useful for such different work?

A chronology of inventions can make the answer seem inevitable: computers arrived, networks grew, data accumulated, and deeper networks became large models. That account leaves the difficult questions unanswered. How does a model use the data? Why does what it learns transfer? What does additional computation improve? These questions are connected, but answering one does not settle the others.

This is an introductory narrative review of selected literature, from computability to pretrained language models in the early 2020s, organized around problems rather than dates. It examines a set of technical relationships leading to LLMs, without attempting a complete history of AI or a ranking of recent models. Symbolic reasoning, search, and other statistical methods have continuing research traditions of their own; the selection here does not imply their replacement by neural networks.

## Computability and learning: two meanings of generality

The Turing machine gives computation a precise description. Finite control states, a readable and writable tape, and transition rules specify how a process proceeds. Once a machine's rules can be encoded as another machine's input, one apparatus can simulate different computations: the essential idea of a universal Turing machine. [Turing, 1936/1937](https://londmathsoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1112/plms/s2-42.1.230).

Here, universality means that the machine can execute a computable procedure when supplied with the appropriate program. It leaves the origin of that program to the designer. A sorting procedure can be written out completely. Recognizing sarcasm is harder: the same word can express opposing attitudes in different contexts, and a longer keyword list may not resolve the ambiguity.

Machine learning lets a program acquire some of its behavior through learning. Designers choose a model, input representation, and objective; training adjusts the model using data or interaction experience. That experience can come from different sources, supporting different forms of learning.

Rosenblatt's perceptron investigated learning to classify. In the familiar supervised formulation, training examples supply correct categories, and classification errors guide weight updates. Samuel's checkers program combined search, position memory, and learning an evaluation function, using information such as scores backed up through search to improve its assessment of positions. It embodied early reinforcement-learning ideas without relying on human labels for the best move at every step; its learning was more involved than simply rewarding wins and penalizing losses. Both learned from experience, but used different feedback. [Rosenblatt, 1958](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0042519), [Samuel, 1959](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5392560).

In text classification, word counts might become numerical features, from which a model learns to predict categories. Adjustable values are parameters; a loss measures disagreement with a target. Training adjusts parameters, while inference uses the trained model to process inputs. Generalization is the harder requirement: the model must remain useful on unseen examples. Memorizing training texts and their labels does not establish that ability.

Universal computation and a general-purpose model therefore concern different things. One asks which programs an apparatus can execute; the other asks which tasks a learned set of parameters can accommodate. Large models are still implemented as programs running under finite time and memory. They expand the range of behaviors learned from data and reused across tasks. Turing's work provides a theoretical starting point, but does not by itself explain their practical capabilities.

## Representation learning: using combinations never seen before

Learning depends heavily on how inputs are represented. If text is represented only through word counts, “cats chase dogs” and “dogs chase cats” receive the same representation, losing who chases whom. Handcrafted features can work well, but restrict the information available to what their designer selected. Representation learning lets a model form useful intermediate features during training.

Neural networks provide one trainable approach. A common feed-forward layer is:

<Equation label="A feed-forward representation transformation">

**h = σ(Wx + b)**

</Equation>

Here x is the input vector, W and b are learned weights and biases, σ is a nonlinear function, and h is the resulting representation. Multiple layers can train together against one loss. Backpropagation applies the chain rule through the computation graph to calculate derivatives; an optimizer uses them to update parameters. Rumelhart and colleagues demonstrated how this procedure can learn internal representations. [Rumelhart et al., 1986](https://www.nature.com/articles/323533a0). The [Vector](/en/concepts/vector/) and [Matrix](/en/concepts/matrix/) chapters develop these numerical objects and transformations.

Multiple trainable layers are central to deep learning. For language, however, sparsity explains their value more clearly than depth alone. Traditional n-gram models estimate continuations from a limited window of words. Longer windows admit far more combinations than any corpus can cover. A model needs to apply experience from some sentences to others.

Bengio and colleagues jointly learned continuous word representations and sequence probabilities, allowing nearby representations to share statistical information. Word representation and prediction became parts of the same learning problem. [Bengio et al., 2003](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v3/bengio03a.html). Mapping discrete items to vectors is called embedding. Individual coordinates need not have named meanings; useful relationships can be distributed across them.

For example, patterns learned from “the cat sleeps on the sofa” might help with “the dog sleeps on the sofa.” This illustrates the purpose of shared representations; actual transfer depends on the data and objective. Representation learning reduces the need to memorize combinations individually, while leaving training to determine which distinctions are retained and which are lost.

## Language prediction as a reusable training task

Representation learning needs an objective. For language, predicting what follows a passage is one practical choice. Shannon's treatment of message sources and statistical approximations to English showed how symbol sequences could be described probabilistically, with entropy measuring average uncertainty. [Shannon, 1948](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf). This makes uncertainty something that can be estimated and compared.

Modern language models usually divide text into tokens: words, subwords, or other encoded units. An autoregressive model factors sequence probability as:

<Equation label="Autoregressive sequence probability">

**Pθ(x₁, …, xₜ) = ∏ᵢ₌₁ᵗ Pθ(xᵢ | x₁, …, xᵢ₋₁)**

</Equation>

Here xᵢ is token i and θ denotes parameters. The model estimates the next-token distribution from preceding text; training typically reduces the negative log probability assigned to the actual continuation. Text supplies its own prediction targets, allowing training on material without manually assigned task labels. Constructing supervision from the data itself is called self-supervised learning. [Probability Distribution](/en/concepts/probability/) introduces the relevant foundations.

Prediction can place varied demands on context processing. Completing a sentence may require local grammar, resolving a pronoun, following the topic, or using factual knowledge. This suggests an explanation for transfer: a prediction task spanning diverse texts can train representations useful elsewhere. The objective alone does not prove that these capabilities will develop; downstream experiments must test that possibility.

Pretraining followed by fine-tuning turns reuse into a training procedure: first learn from broad data, then update parameters using a target task's examples. GPT and BERT illustrate different choices. GPT uses generative language pretraining; BERT learns bidirectional contextual representations with objectives including masked language modeling. Both reuse pretraining, but their accessible context, objectives, and applications differ. [Radford et al., 2018](https://cdn.openai.com/research-covers/language-unsupervised/language_understanding_paper.pdf), [Devlin et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805).

GPT-3 investigated another use: placing a task description or a few examples in the input and generating a result. Its in-context evaluations did not update parameters for those tasks. [Brown et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165). For sentiment classification, fine-tuning changes parameters using labeled examples; in-context learning supplies sentence–label examples in the prompt for the model to continue. Both can improve performance, but differ in cost, persistence, and failure modes.

Together, these studies extend language modeling from a standalone task into a reusable training basis. They also expose a gap between training and use. A common, fluent continuation may contain false claims. Better prediction loss alone does not establish reliable completion of work requested by a user.

## Computing over context: compression and selective access

A training objective still needs a structure that uses context effectively. Recurrent networks read a sequence step by step, preserving history in an updated state. Sutskever and colleagues used an encoder to read input and a decoder to generate output. [Sutskever et al., 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.3215). This accommodates different input and output lengths in translation, but asks a fixed-length representation to retain the information needed for generation.

Attention changes access to that information. Bahdanau and colleagues' translation model weights source positions separately for each target word, combining information relevant to that step. [Bahdanau et al., 2014/2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.0473). It can consult representations near the start of the source while translating its end, instead of relying entirely on one final compressed representation. “Attention” here names a learned weighting operation.

The Transformer organizes sequence computation around attention; its original architecture has an encoder and a decoder. [Vaswani et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762). Self-attention exchanges information within a sequence: inputs are projected into queries Q, keys K, and values V. Q–K [dot products](/en/concepts/dot-product/) yield scores; scaling and [Softmax](/en/concepts/softmax/) produce weights for combining V. Autoregressive models use a causal mask to exclude future tokens. Position handling, feed-forward layers, residual connections, and normalization complete the architecture.

Comparing these approaches reveals two related benefits. Attention improves access to information; the Transformer makes training computation across positions easier to parallelize. Both help with context use and the feasibility of larger training runs. The costs remain concrete: standard full attention handles a number of position pairs quadratic in sequence length, and autoregressive generation typically produces tokens sequentially. [Self Attention](/en/concepts/self-attention/) develops the mechanism further.

## Conditions for scaling: data, hardware, and training budgets

Architecture alone cannot explain why large models became practical. Data availability, organization, and affordable computation change what an algorithm can achieve.

The Internet and the Web have different roles. The Internet connects computers through packet switching and communication protocols; the Web organizes documents over that infrastructure through addresses, hypertext, and access protocols. ARPANET and the adoption of TCP/IP contributed to interconnection, while CERN's Web work advanced document publication and access. [Leiner et al., Internet history](https://www.internetsociety.org/resources/doc/2017/brief-history-internet/), [CERN, history of the Web](https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/). Accumulating digital content expanded potential corpora, but raw pages still needed considerable processing before becoming training data.

MapReduce studied large-scale data processing on clusters; ImageNet demonstrated the collection and organization of a large labeled image dataset. They illustrate processing capacity and data organization respectively. [Dean and Ghemawat, 2004](https://research.google/pubs/mapreduce-simplified-data-processing-on-large-clusters/), [Deng et al., 2009](https://www-cs.stanford.edu/groups/vision/pdf/ImageNet_CVPR2009.pdf). Distributing work across nodes does not fix duplicates, incorrect labels, or uneven coverage. Those remain data-pipeline concerns.

AlexNet supplies a concrete example of these conditions meeting: a deep convolutional network, substantial labeled data, and GPU computation produced strong ImageNet classification results. [Krizhevsky et al., 2012](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/4824-imagenet-classification-with-deep-convolutional-neural-networks). Convolution exploits local image structure, while GPUs support parallel numerical work. The result demonstrated the practical value of deep representations, though convolution alone cannot explain the development of language models. The broader lesson is to examine algorithms, data, and hardware together.

Scaling research makes their relationship a measurable allocation problem. Kaplan and colleagues studied empirical relationships between language-model loss and model size, data, and compute. Hoffmann and colleagues' Chinchilla work further argued for coordinated growth of parameters and training data under compute constraints. [Kaplan et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361), [Hoffmann et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556). Enlarging a model is not always the best use of a budget; how much data it can train on also matters.

These findings inform planning within the scope of their experimental evidence. Lower prediction loss, better task performance, and favorable deployment economics require separate evaluation. Calling all three “a stronger model” hides consequential differences.

An LLM generally has substantial parameters trained on large-scale text and related data, without a permanent numerical threshold. The term foundation model emphasizes broad training followed by adaptation to many downstream tasks. [Bommasani et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07258). This distinction directs attention to what compute buys: reusable capabilities, and the adaptation costs that remain for particular tasks.

## From models to agents: what must be evaluated?

After pretraining, a model still needs to accommodate user requests. InstructGPT used demonstrations, preference comparisons, and reinforcement learning from human feedback to improve instruction following. [Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155). Such subsequent training changes output preferences without guaranteeing factual support. A response users prefer and a response they can verify require separate scrutiny.

External information access is another concern. Retrieval-augmented generation supplies retrieved material to a generator, allowing it to use records beyond its parameters. [Lewis et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401). Learned parameters, current context, and external sources play different roles: parameters supply trained capabilities, context carries the current task, and external records provide evidence on demand. Both retrieval accuracy and faithful use of retrieved material affect the result.

Tasks involving file changes or queries also need outputs connected to actions. ReAct explored interleaving reasoning and action. [Yao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629). It is one route to agents using language models; agent research itself predates LLMs. The immediate consequence is that evaluation must inspect what happened in the environment as well as the generated text.

Suppose a user asks for a bug fix. A plausible patch is only part of the work. Completion depends on whether files were written, relevant tests passed, and new problems were introduced. When a tool fails, the system needs to retry, revise its approach, or stop. The model proposes requests; the runtime validates arguments, checks permissions, executes operations, and records results. [Tool Calling](/en/concepts/tool-calling/) and [Agent Loop](/en/concepts/agent-loop/) examine this boundary and multistep execution.

Different evaluations serve different purposes. Prediction loss assesses language modeling, task metrics measure particular capabilities, and system evaluation checks outcomes, costs, and recovery. The first two inform system design; the complete process still needs validation on actual tasks.

## Discussion and conclusion

Read together, the selected studies suggest an account based on interacting constraints. Computability supplies a formal basis; learning methods specify how experience changes behavior; representations and architectures determine which relationships are accessible; data and hardware constrain how far training can go. Pretraining turns that investment into reusable parameters, while in-context learning and subsequent adaptation change how those parameters are used. This is a synthesis of the selected literature, without implying that every research tradition must converge on the same model.

Two questions remain. The relationship between prediction performance and specific capabilities needs task-level investigation and examination of failures; one score cannot capture the whole picture. Turning capabilities expressed in text into reliable actions requires system-level validation. Larger training runs and better runtimes address different parts of the problem, and engineering has to consider both.

Later chapters develop the pieces needed to investigate these questions. Vectors, matrices, and dot products explain representation transformations; probability and Softmax explain prediction and attention weights; tool calling and agent loops explain participation in multistep work. Following these prerequisites makes it possible to reason about where an improvement comes from and under which conditions it fails.
