# Vector

## Abstract

Vectors can describe displacement in space or represent features of text, images, and model states. These uses share operations such as addition, scaling, and comparison, while the meaning of their values comes from different sources. This chapter develops the distinction between vectors and coordinates through two-dimensional geometry, then examines length, linear combinations, and normalization. It compares handcrafted features, static word embeddings, and contextual representations to ask how numbers become useful representations, and what additional evidence is needed to interpret similarity or meaning.

## How can a sentence become a set of numbers?

Suppose a knowledge base needs to answer “What if I forget my password?” Its documentation might instead describe “resetting account credentials.” Exact word matching could miss that passage. Vector retrieval attempts to encode questions and passages numerically, then use relationships between their representations to find candidates.

Two issues deserve separate attention. Vectors supply the mathematical operations: addition, scaling, and distance. The encoder determines how content enters that structure: which distinctions survive and which texts appear close. Replacing words with numbers does not automatically produce semantic retrieval.

The same distinction applies inside language models. An embedding, a hidden state at a particular layer, and a passage vector used for retrieval can all be written as lists of numbers without serving the same purpose. Studying vectors gives us their common mathematical structure; the model and training objective then inform the interpretation.

<Aside kind="context" title="Reading prerequisites">

Basic arithmetic is enough. We begin with two- and three-dimensional examples; higher-dimensional representations use the same operations. Matrices and dot products are developed in later chapters.

</Aside>

## Vectors, coordinates, and bases

### Starting with displacement

Moving three units right and two units up describes a displacement `v = [3, 2]`. In a diagram, we commonly place the arrow's tail at the origin and its head at `(3, 2)`. Moving the whole arrow elsewhere without changing its length or direction leaves the displacement unchanged.

This connects points and vectors while distinguishing their roles. A point specifies a position; a displacement vector describes a change in position. Once an origin is chosen, a vector from that origin can represent a point's position, so both often use the same coordinates. Model vectors are likewise drawn as points when discussing distance or clustering.

### Coordinates depend on the measuring system

The standard basis vectors are `e₁ = [1, 0]` and `e₂ = [0, 1]`. They specify the directions and units used to measure the displacement:

<Equation label="Coordinates are coefficients in a basis">

**v = 3e₁ + 2e₂**

</Equation>

The numbers 3 and 2 are combination coefficients. With a different basis, `b₁ = [2, 0]` and `b₂ = [0, 1]`, the same vector is `1.5b₁ + 2b₂`, with coordinates `[1.5, 2]`. The displacement stays the same; the measuring system changes.

In a finite-dimensional space, a basis must express every vector, and must do so uniquely. The first property is spanning; linear independence ensures the second. Two arrows on one line cannot cover the entire plane through scaling and addition.

<Aside kind="definition" title="Notation used here">

Unless stated otherwise, we use the standard basis of perpendicular unit vectors. `[x, y]` abbreviates the coordinates; `[x, y]ᵀ` explicitly denotes a column vector. More general vector spaces can contain objects such as functions. This chapter focuses on real coordinate spaces used in models.

</Aside>

This distinction also matters for learned representations. Two models producing the same number of coordinates need not organize those coordinates compatibly. Without joint training or explicit alignment, their vectors may not support direct comparison.

## Combining information through addition and scaling

Vector addition adds corresponding coordinates. For `u = [3, 2]` and `v = [-1, 1]`, the sum is `[2, 3]`. Geometrically, follow u, then v from its endpoint; the total displacement is their sum. The interpretation also makes clear why corresponding coordinates must have compatible meanings.

<Equation label="Vector addition and scalar multiplication">

**[x₁, y₁] + [x₂, y₂] = [x₁ + x₂, y₁ + y₂]**

**a[x, y] = [ax, ay]**

</Equation>

The scalar a is a number. Multiplying `[3, 2]` by `0.5` halves its length; multiplying by `-1` reverses its direction; multiplying by zero produces the zero vector. In general, length changes by a factor of `|a|`. The zero vector has no defined direction, an important exception when discussing angles and normalization.

Combining addition and scaling gives a linear combination. For the same u and v, `0.25u + 0.75v = [0, 1.25]`. The coefficients are nonnegative and sum to one, so the result lies on the segment between their endpoints: a weighted average. General linear combinations allow negative coefficients and do not require their sum to be one, so they need not remain on that segment.

Weighted aggregation in attention, some methods for averaging passage representations, and addition in residual connections all use these operations. Mathematical validity alone does not establish a semantic interpretation. Adding two word vectors is straightforward; whether the result expresses a useful combination of meanings depends on the learned structure. Vector addition does not guarantee that language meanings combine in the same way.

## Length, distance, and normalization

In standard two-dimensional coordinates, the vector and its axis components form a right triangle. The Pythagorean theorem gives its Euclidean length, or L2 norm:

<Equation label="Euclidean norm">

**‖v‖₂ = √(x² + y²)**

**‖v‖₂ = √(v₁² + ⋯ + v_d²)**

</Equation>

The second expression extends the definition to d dimensions. For `[3, 2]`, the length is `√13 ≈ 3.606`. Viewing vectors as points, their Euclidean distance is the norm of their difference. The difference between `[3, 2]` and `[1, 1]` is `[2, 1]`, giving distance `√5`.

Whether length has a useful application-level meaning depends on the representation. Coordinates representing height and weight mix different units when squared and added. Changing height from meters to centimeters can substantially change the result. Feature scales and the intended comparison therefore need attention. Even for learned coordinates, greater length should not be interpreted as greater importance or confidence without evidence.

When a comparison concerns direction, a nonzero vector can be L2-normalized:

<Equation label="Scaling a nonzero vector to unit length">

**v̂ = v / ‖v‖₂**

</Equation>

Normalizing `[3, 2]` gives approximately `[0.832, 0.555]`. Direction is retained, but original length is lost: `[6, 4]` gives the same result. Normalization is consequently a tradeoff, useful when length differences should be ignored rather than a universal improvement. [Dot Product](/en/concepts/dot-product/) explains directional comparison after normalization.

<Aside kind="tip" title="A different basis can require a different length calculation">

The earlier displacement had coordinates `[1.5, 2]` in the new basis, but its length is not `2.5`: the first basis vector has length 2. The ordinary sum-of-squares formula assumes an orthonormal basis. A general basis requires accounting for its vectors' lengths and angles.

</Aside>

## From handcrafted features to contextual representations

### Where relationships in a representation come from

A simple text representation gives each vocabulary word a coordinate and records its frequency. This is interpretable, but does not automatically capture related meanings. A one-hot representation of an individual word places a 1 in its coordinate and zeros elsewhere. Every pair of distinct words then has Euclidean distance `√2`. “Cat” and “dog” are no closer than “cat” and “contract.”

Neural language models seek to learn useful relationships from data. Bengio and colleagues jointly trained word representations and language prediction so that contexts could share statistical information. Mikolov and colleagues investigated efficient continuous word representations and evaluated them on word relationship tasks. [Bengio et al., 2003](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v3/bengio03a.html), [Mikolov et al., 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781).

Closeness in these representations comes from training, not from the use of vectors alone. A model may make substitution, topic, or grammatical relationships easier to identify while also retaining biases in the data. Individual coordinates rarely offer a sufficient explanation; useful information can be distributed across them.

### A word's representation and its representation in a sentence

Static word embeddings typically assign a fixed vector to each word. Yet “bank” can occur in descriptions of financial services or riverbanks. Contextual models form internal representations using surrounding text. BERT demonstrates a use of such representations through bidirectional contextual pretraining. [Devlin et al., 2018/2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805).

When a paper mentions a vector, ask which one: an input embedding, a contextual state at some layer, or an aggregated representation of a passage? All can have the same dimension while differing in their information sources and training purposes. A hidden state useful for predicting subsequent tokens is not automatically a good retrieval vector.

## Minimal implementation and checkable results

The implementation stores two coordinates in an object. `add` and `scale` follow the component-wise definitions, while `magnitude` uses `Math.hypot` for Euclidean length.

<CodeStep title="vec2.ts">

~~~ts
type Vec2 = {
  x: number;
  y: number;
};

function add(a: Vec2, b: Vec2): Vec2 {
  return {
    x: a.x + b.x,
    y: a.y + b.y
  };
}

function scale(v: Vec2, scalar: number): Vec2 {
  return {
    x: v.x * scalar,
    y: v.y * scalar
  };
}

function magnitude(v: Vec2): number {
  return Math.hypot(v.x, v.y);
}
~~~

</CodeStep>

For `u = { x: 3, y: 2 }` and `v = { x: -1, y: 1 }`, `add(u, v)` returns `{ x: 2, y: 3 }`; `scale(u, 0.5)` returns `{ x: 1.5, y: 1 }`; and `magnitude(u)` is approximately `3.606`. These can be checked against both the explanation and the experiment.

The code assumes finite real coordinates. A normalization function would need to handle zero length explicitly instead of dividing by zero. With higher-dimensional arrays, addition also requires equal lengths. Matching dimensions make an operation formally possible; compatibility between the models and coordinate conventions remains a separate responsibility of the caller.

## Discussion: what vectors let us conclude

In vector retrieval, the encoder and comparison method jointly determine candidate results. Replacing an embedding model requires checking compatibility between question and document representations even if dimensions match. Changing normalization changes how length affects ranking. Both choices need evaluation on actual retrieval tasks.

Transformer representations undergo transformations and weighted combinations, with additions through residual paths. Vector operations describe these processes precisely, but one coordinate or similarity calculation rarely explains an entire model decision. [Vaswani et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762). For an agent, retrieving a related passage supplies candidate evidence; its source, freshness, and support for the answer still need examination.

This chapter connects representations with operations: coordinates depend on conventions, length and direction preserve different information, and addition and scaling combine representations. [Matrix](/en/concepts/matrix/) develops transformations, while [Dot Product](/en/concepts/dot-product/) develops comparison. Asking where the numbers came from and what an operation preserves is more useful than treating every vector as an unexplained container of “meaning.”
